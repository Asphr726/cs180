Project 4

Stitching Photo Mosaics

Photos taken from one spot while the camera turns are related by a homography, a 3 × 3 matrix. Part A recovers it from hand-picked correspondences, then uses it to rectify planes, warp images into a shared frame, feather them into mosaics, and wrap a 117° view onto a cylinder. Part B replaces manual correspondences with feature matching and robust estimation.

Overview

If the camera only rotates about its optical center, every ray it records is still the same ray in the next photo. Only the image plane has turned. Two pinhole images of the same rays differ by a projective map of the plane, \(p' \sim Hp\), and depth stops mattering.

\[ \begin{bmatrix} wx' \\ wy' \\ w \end{bmatrix} = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} a & b & c \\ d & e & f \\ g & h & 1 \end{bmatrix}}_{H\;=\;K R K^{-1}} \begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad x' = \frac{ax+by+c}{gx+hy+1},\quad y' = \frac{dx+ey+f}{gx+hy+1} \]

The same equation covers a second case: any photo of a flat surface. That is what rectification uses. The page follows the pipeline in order: shoot (A.1), estimate \(H\) (A.2), warp with two samplers (A.3), blend (A.4), and go past what a plane can hold with a cylinder (A.5). Interactive figures are marked in their captions.

Part A

Image Warping and Mosaicing

Every Part A result comes from code/main_a.py with NumPy, and OpenCV is used only for file I/O, color conversion, and a distance transform. The extra figures (the equal-weight comparison, intermediate canvases, timings) come from a separate script that calls the same functions. The homography solver, both samplers, the blender, and the cylindrical projection are written by hand; no findHomography, warpPerspective or remap. Correspondences were clicked with the course correspondence tool, 6 to 13 per pair, and saved as JSON.

A handheld camera does not rotate exactly about its optical center. Distant scenery tolerates that: a few centimeters of drift is tiny next to a mountain kilometers away. Nearby objects do not. Part A 2

Recover Homographies

Each correspondence \((x,y)\mapsto(u,v)\) gives two equations that are linear in the nine entries of \(H\) once the denominator is multiplied out. Stacking \(n\) pairs gives a \(2n\times 9\) system.

\[ \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} x_1 & y_1 & 1 & 0 & 0 & 0 & -u_1x_1 & -u_1y_1 & -u_1 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & x_1 & y_1 & 1 & -v_1x_1 & -v_1y_1 & -v_1 \\ \vdots & & & & & & & & \vdots \\ x_n & y_n & 1 & 0 & 0 & 0 & -u_nx_n & -u_ny_n & -u_n \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & x_n & y_n & 1 & -v_nx_n & -v_ny_n & -v_n \end{bmatrix}}_{A\;(2n\times 9)} \begin{bmatrix} h_{11} \\ h_{12} \\ \vdots \\ h_{33} \end{bmatrix} = \mathbf 0 \]

With four points the system has an exact one-dimensional null space. With more, noise makes it full rank, so computeH solves the least-squares problem \(\min\lVert Ah\rVert\) subject to \(\lVert h\rVert=1\). The answer is the right singular vector of \(A\) with the smallest singular value. It is then rescaled so \(h_{33}=1\), the same eight-parameter form as the spec's \(Ah=b\). The fit itself differs slightly, because no entry is fixed while solving. On these pairs the two agree to within 0.001 px. 

def computeH(img1_pts, img2_pts):
    img1_pts_norm, T1 = normalizePoints(img1_pts)   # centroid at 0, mean distance √2
    img2_pts_norm, T2 = normalizePoints(img2_pts)
    A = []                                          # two rows per correspondence
    for (x, y), (u, v) in zip(img1_pts_norm, img2_pts_norm):
        A.append([x, y, 1, 0, 0, 0, -u*x, -u*y, -u])
        A.append([0, 0, 0, x, y, 1, -v*x, -v*y, -v])
    _, _, Vt = np.linalg.svd(np.asarray(A))
    H = np.linalg.inv(T2) @ Vt[-1].reshape(3, 3) @ T1   # undo the normalization
    return H / H[2, 2]

Why normalize first

In raw pixels the columns of \(A\) mix 1, coordinates near 500, and products near 250,000. The ratio between the largest and the eighth singular value, which sets how much click noise is amplified into \(h\), has a median of \(4.0\times10^{6}\) over the 14 pairs. After moving each point set's centroid to the origin and scaling its mean distance to \(\sqrt2\) (Hartley normalization), the median is 6.6. The fit is done there and mapped back with \(H=T_2^{-1}\hat H\,T_1\).

Part A 3

Warp the Images

Warping is done backwards. The four corners of the source go through \(H\) to find the output bounding box. Then every output pixel is sent through \(H^{-1}\) to a real-valued location in the source, and a sampler decides what color lives there. Going backwards means every output pixel gets exactly one value, with no holes. Pixel \((0,0)\) is the center of the first pixel, so a point is inside the source when \(-0.5\le x<w-0.5\) for nearest neighbor and \(0\le x\le w-1\) for bilinear. Output pixels that land outside get alpha 0.

Nearest neighbor

x_nn = np.floor(x_src + 0.5).astype(int)
y_nn = np.floor(y_src + 0.5).astype(int)
out[valid] = im[y_nn, x_nn]

Bilinear

x0, y0 = floor(x), floor(y);  dx, dy = x-x0, y-y0
out = (1-dx)(1-dy) I00 + dx(1-dy) I10
    + (1-dx) dy   I01 + dx dy     I11

Try it: rectify by dragging four corners

Rectification is a homography with a made-up target. Choose four points that should be the corners of a rectangle, send them to the pixel centers \((0,0),(w-1,0),(w-1,h-1),(0,h-1)\) of a \(w\times h\) output, and warp. The demo below runs the same math in the browser. It solves the 8 × 8 system for the map from the output rectangle back to your quadrilateral and samples the photo.

The board's true aspect ratio is unknown. I estimated it as 1.36 from the photo itself: with the focal length from the EXIF data, the corner homography must factor into a rotation and a translation, and that fixes the width-to-height ratio (it changes by less than 1% between f = 700 and 850 px). The target rectangle is 700 × 515.

The placard is nearly square: the same focal-length argument gives an aspect ratio of 1.02 (0.99 to 1.03 for f between 650 and 800 px). The target is 600 × 590. Every row is stretched about 2× vertically (2.4× at the far top edge, 1.8× at the near bottom), so the samplers have to invent more pixels here than for the board.

Speed against quality

Both samplers cost the same inverse mapping. The difference is the lookup: nearest neighbor reads one pixel, bilinear reads four and blends them. That makes bilinear about 3× slower here, and both scale linearly with the number of output pixels. Nearest neighbor copies each source pixel to a whole block of output, so lines that should be straight become staircases and fine repeated patterns shimmer. Bilinear trades that for a slight blur, because it low-passes the image at subpixel positions. For mosaics, where every image is resampled and then averaged, bilinear's blur is invisible and the staircases are not, so every mosaic uses bilinear.

Part A 4

Blend the Images into a Mosaic

Correspondences only exist between neighbors, so stitch chains them. The middle image is the reference, which keeps the worst stretching symmetric. Every other image reaches it by multiplying the neighbor homographies in between, inverting where the chain runs the other way.

  1. Chain to the reference.\(H_{i\to r}=H_{i+1\to r}\,H_{i\to i+1}\) on the left of the reference and \(H_{i\to r}=H_{i-1\to r}\,H_{i-1\to i}^{-1}\) on the right.
  2. Grow the canvas.Images are added outward from the reference. Each step warps the next image's corners, takes the union with the current mosaic's box, and pre-multiplies a translation so the new top-left is \((0,0)\).
  3. Warp with alpha.The new image and the mosaic so far are both resampled into the new canvas. Color is premultiplied by alpha first, so empty pixels never bleed into edges.
  4. Feather.At each step there are two sources, the new image and the mosaic so far. Each gets a weight from a distance transform of its own coverage: 0 at the edge, rising linearly to 1 at its deepest pixel. Each output pixel is \((w_1c_1+w_2c_2)/(w_1+w_2)\). The mosaic counts as one region, so its weight comes from its outer boundary, not from the photos inside it.
def feather_weights(mask):
    padded = np.pad(mask.astype(np.uint8), 1)           # the border counts as an edge
    dist = cv.distanceTransform(padded, cv.DIST_L2, 5)[1:-1, 1:-1]
    return dist / dist.max()

weight = alpha * feather_weights(alpha > 0)              # inside stitchImages
sources.append(np.dstack((color * weight[:, :, None], weight, alpha)))

Equal weights versus feathering

My first version averaged every overlapping pixel with equal weight. Inside an overlap that is fine. At the overlap's border the weight jumps from ½ to 1, and any difference in exposure becomes a visible step. Auto-exposure shifts a little from frame to frame, so each image's rectangle shows up in the sky. Drag the divider to compare.

Because each weight reaches 0 exactly where its image ends, nothing changes abruptly at a border. Inside an overlap, whichever source is deeper inside its own coverage dominates, so the transition is spread over the full overlap width instead of happening at one line. Feathering does not fix misregistration, though. If the two images disagree about where something is, a gentle crossfade still shows both copies.

What went right, and what did not

Feathering removed every exposure seam in the three mosaics. The sky of the ridge and the ceiling of the room are now continuous. What remains is geometry. My first sequoia mosaic had a doubled canopy at the upper right. Its six points for photos 2 → 3 all sat in one band across the middle of the overlap. \(H\) fit them to 0.38 px, but it had to extrapolate into the canopy, where an independent feature-matching check put it off by about 11 px on average and up to 74 px. A low error at the clicked points says nothing about the parts of the overlap without any. I re-clicked 13 points spread over the whole overlap, including the canopy (0.96 px), and the ghost is gone. The rest is parallax from handheld drift, which no homography can remove. The lower branches of the near pine in the middle of the ridge mosaic smear where neighboring photos overlap. In the room, the chairs and the right-hand table, a meter or two from the camera, show faint doubles. Five ridge photos fill 72% of the planar canvas; nine fill only 49%, and the outermost trees are smeared several-fold. Beyond about five photos the plane is the wrong surface, which is where A.5 starts.

Part A 5

Bells & Whistles

A cylinder of radius \(f\) around the camera keeps every horizontal direction at the same scale. Position along the cylinder is proportional to angle, so a 360° pan would only need a strip \(2\pi f\) wide. Each image is projected onto it by inverse sampling, computing a source position for every cylinder pixel, with the same bilinear sampler and premultiplied alpha as before.

\[ \theta=\frac{u}{f},\quad x = f\tan\theta,\quad y = \frac{v}{\cos\theta} \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad u = f\arctan\frac{x}{f},\quad v = \frac{f\,y}{\sqrt{x^2+f^2}} \]

After this warp a pure pan is a pure horizontal shift, so each neighbor pair is aligned by the mean offset of its correspondences, mapped onto the cylinder, and the same stitch and feathering run with fit=fitTranslation in place of computeH. The one free parameter is \(f\) in pixels. I chose it as the value that makes the cylinder correspondences most consistent with pure translation.

The residual drops steeply below 700 px and is nearly flat from 800 to 1200 px, with its minimum of 5.49 px at \(f=1010\) px. For a 512 × 683 image that is a horizontal field of view of 28°, a 35 mm equivalent of about 51 mm, though the flat stretch spans roughly 41–61 mm. That fits a phone's 2× setting (48–52 mm) much better than its 1× main lens (24–26 mm, which would be \(f\approx 470\)–\(510\) px). Most of the floor of about 5 px comes from the last pair, 8 → 9 (13.8 px on its own), where the sign and railing shift against the mountains. The other seven pairs leave about 2.7 px, the small tilt and roll between shots that a translation cannot model. Without 8 → 9 the minimum moves to 900 px, still inside the flat stretch.

\(f=400\) px\(f=700\) px\(f=1010\) px (fitted)Photo 5456 px wide491 px wide502 px wide

A smaller radius bends the photo harder. At \(f=400\) px the top and bottom edges bow into arcs and the 512 px width shrinks to 456 px, because the edges are squeezed by \(\arctan(x/f)\). The trunks stay vertical at every radius, since a cylinder maps vertical lines to vertical lines. At the fitted radius the photo is barely changed (502 px wide), and that small correction is enough to make the nine photos chain without kinks. The full panorama is at the top of the page.

Plane versus cylinder

The planar mosaic of all nine photos needs a 4576 × 2496 canvas and leaves half of it empty. The cylindrical one is 2062 × 755 with every photo at its native scale. The cost is that straight lines that are not vertical now curve: the horizon stays level only because the pan was nearly level. Its staircase top and bottom edges are the vertical drift of a handheld pan, which a translation-only model leaves in rather than spreads out.

Part B.1

Harris Corner Detection

Harris detects local intensity changes in two directions. I use the supplied single-scale detector, discard a 20 px boundary, and keep 200 points with adaptive non-maximal suppression. For each point, its suppression radius is the distance to the nearest point satisfying fᵢ < 0.9 fⱼ. The largest radii survive: a strong cluster cannot consume the entire feature budget.

Part B.2

Feature Descriptor Extraction

Each feature samples a blurred 40 × 40 neighborhood on an axis-aligned 8 × 8 grid with 5 px spacing. A 17 × 17 Gaussian prefilter reduces aliasing. Subtracting the mean and dividing by the standard deviation removes additive brightness and positive contrast changes. Flat patches remain zero and cannot pass an ambiguous nearest-neighbor test. These are simplified MOPS-style patches: the baseline has neither scale nor rotation invariance.

Part B.3

Feature Matching

For each descriptor, I find its two nearest neighbors by Euclidean distance and accept the best only when d₁/d₂ < 0.8. This measures distinctiveness: a small distance is not enough if another candidate is almost as close. The threshold uses distances, not squared distances (the equivalent SSD ratio is 0.64). Repeated foliage and furniture can still produce false matches, so this is an appearance filter before geometric verification.

Part B.4

RANSAC for Robust Homography

I sample four correspondences for 1,000 RANSAC trials, reject degenerate configurations, estimate a normalized DLT homography, and count forward reprojection errors below 5 px. The largest consensus is refitted, and membership is recomputed against the returned matrix. The random seed is 180. Adjacent transforms are composed into the middle image’s coordinate frame; the original Part A inverse warping and feathering then produce the mosaics.

What automation can and cannot fix

Hundreds of candidates replace a few hand-picked points, but a low fitting error only describes the selected correspondences. Nearby pine branches and chairs can move relative to the background because the camera translates slightly; one homography cannot align both depths. Repeated texture can bias the consensus, while composing adjacent transforms propagates estimation error toward the outer frames. Feathering hides exposure boundaries but cannot correct a geometric double edge.

Part B.5

Bells & Whistles

The extension estimates a dominant direction from the image gradient after Gaussian smoothing with σ = 4.5 px. Rotating the sampling grid into that local frame gives corresponding neighborhoods a common orientation. The descriptor remains 8 × 8 with the same spacing, blur, normalization and ratio threshold; the baseline remains available unchanged.

For a controlled test, I rotate the first image of each scene by known angles using the handwritten inverse warper. Both methods use the same detected points, a 30 px source boundary and a 30 px erosion of the target validity mask to exclude padded borders. A match is correct when its position agrees with the known rotation within 5 px. This evaluates matching directly, independently of a RANSAC estimate.

Orientation is an estimate too

Direction normalization changes the trade-off rather than guaranteeing a win on every image. A corner can have a weak or ambiguous smoothed gradient; small localization changes then rotate the sampling window substantially. Interpolation and changing corner detections also limit a synthetic rotation experiment. The table reports every tested angle and scene, including failures, rather than selecting only a favorable example. Across the three scenes at 45°, the axis-aligned baseline retains only 0–1 correct matches, while direction normalization retains over 100 in every scene. At 0°, it retains fewer correct matches than the baseline: direction estimation has a cost even without rotation. Scale and projective distortions remain outside this extension.
