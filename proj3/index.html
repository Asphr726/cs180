Project 3

Fun with Flow Models

A pretrained text-to-image flow model, taken apart and put to new uses: noising and denoising by hand, Euler sampling, classifier-free guidance, image editing, and two kinds of optical illusion. Then a small flow-matching model, trained from scratch on MNIST.

From noise to image along a straight line

A flow model learns a velocity field. Start from Gaussian noise at \(t=0\), follow the field, and arrive at an image at \(t=1.\) Training only needs straight lines between noise and data. The model learns the average direction of all the lines that pass through each point.

\[ x_t=(1-t)\,\epsilon+t\,x_1,\qquad \frac{dx_t}{dt}=x_1-\epsilon,\qquad v_\theta(x_t,t)\approx\mathbb{E}\!\left[x_1-\epsilon\mid x_t\right] \]

The demo below is that picture in two dimensions. The data is six Gaussian blobs, two for each of three classes. For this data the optimal velocity has a closed form. It is the posterior-weighted average of each blob's velocity, so no network is needed. The same seeded noise starts every run. Each setting reproduces something from the image experiments below.

  • Time runs from noise to data. \(t=0\) is pure noise and \(t=1\) is a clean image. The model predicts a velocity, not the noise.
  • Two ways to write guidance. Part A uses \(v_c+w(v_c-v_u)\) with \(w=7\). Part B uses \(v_u+\gamma(v_c-v_u)\) with \(\gamma=5\). They agree when \(\gamma=w+1.\)

Part A

Exploring a pretrained flow model

Every experiment in Part A uses PixNerd-XXL-P16-T2I, a pixel-space text-to-image flow model that produces 512 × 512 images. Nothing is trained. Prompts are encoded once with the supplied model.encode, and the empty string serves as the unconditional prompt. All runs use float32 on a T4 GPU with base seed 114 (114 to 118 for the five samples), Euler step \(h=0.02\), and guidance \(w=7\) wherever guidance applies.

Part A 1.1

Noisy training inputs

A training input is a point on the straight line between a noise sample and a clean image. I used one photograph of the Golden Gate Bridge throughout Section 1, center-cropped and resized to 512 × 512 with RGB in \([-1,1]\). One noise tensor \(\epsilon\) is drawn and reused for all three times, so the only thing that changes across the row is \(t.\) 

def interpolate(image, noise, t):
    return (1 - t) * noise + t * image

Part A 1.2

Classical denoising

The classical estimate is \(\hat x_1^{\text{blur}}=G_\sigma(x_t/t).\) Dividing by \(t\) restores the image's amplitude, but it scales the noise as well. What is left is \(x_1+\tfrac{1-t}{t}\,\epsilon\), with noise standard deviation 3, 1, and 1/3 at the three times. For each time I swept σ ∈ {1, 2, 4, 8, 16} and kept the blur closest to the clean image in mean squared error. 

for t,x in noisy.items():
    candidates = {s: gaussian_blur(x/t, [int(6*s+1)]*2, [s,s]) for s in [1.,2.,4.,8.,16.]}
    sigma = min(candidates, key=lambda s: ((candidates[s]-image)**2).mean().item())
    blurred[t] = candidates[sigma]
\(t=0.25\)\(t=0.50\)\(t=0.75\) Noisy \(x_t\) Best blur σ = 8σ = 4σ = 2

The chosen σ halves each time the noise level falls by about three: 8, 4, 2. A wider blur removes more noise and more of the image along with it. MSE picks the point where the two losses balance. At \(t=0.25\) the balance point is a smear. The cables and the far tower are gone, and the sky still carries low-frequency color blotches, because a Gaussian cannot remove noise in the same frequency band as the image. The filter knows nothing about bridges. It treats every frequency above its cutoff as noise.

Part A 1.3

One-step denoising

Rearranging the interpolation gives \(x_1=x_t+(1-t)(x_1-\epsilon)\). Replacing the true velocity with the model's prediction, conditioned on a high quality photo, gives an estimate of the clean image from a single network call. The velocity is added, not subtracted. It points from noise toward data. 

def one_step_denoise(x, t, condition, predict_velocity):
    return x + (1 - t) * predict_velocity(x, t, condition)

Why one step looks blurry at small t

The flow-matching loss is a mean squared error, so the network predicts the conditional mean \(\mathbb{E}[x_1-\epsilon\mid x_t]\), and the one-step estimate is \(\mathbb{E}[x_1\mid x_t]\): the average of every clean image that could have produced this noisy input. At \(t=0.75\) only images very close to the original are consistent with \(x_t\), and the average is sharp. At \(t=0.25\) many images are consistent, and the average blurs whatever they disagree on: the cables, the far shore. It still beats the best Gaussian by 5 to 6 dB at every time, because the model averages over plausible photographs instead of over neighboring pixels.

Part A 1.4

Visualizing velocity

The update the model adds in 1.3 is \((1-t)\hat v_t\). With the saved noise, the update that would recover the original exactly is \((1-t)v_{\text{gt}}=x_1-x_t\). Both are signed images. They share one symmetric scale across all three times, with zero at middle gray. The error map is the per-pixel RMS over RGB of their difference, drawn with viridis from 0 to the largest error over all three times. 

v_t = predict_velocity(x_t, t, condition)
v_gt = clean_image - epsilon
predicted_update = (1 - t) * v_t
ground_truth_update = (1 - t) * v_gt

Both updates subtract the noise

Mid-gray is zero, so both updates read as a photographic negative of the noise laid over a faint bridge. Most of either image is \(-(1-t)\epsilon\). The model has correctly identified the noise to remove. Both shrink as \(t\) grows, because there is less noise left to remove.

The error lives on the edges

The error map lights up along the tower, the cables, and the ridge line, and stays dark on the smooth sky. Where the image is smooth, any plausible photograph agrees, and the conditional mean is right. At edges and fine texture the noisy input does not pin down exactly where the edge lies, so the mean smears it. This is the same disagreement that blurs the one-step estimate. It fades as \(t\) rises and the input becomes less ambiguous.

Part A 1.5

Iterative denoising with Euler steps

Instead of jumping straight to \(t=1\), take a small step along the predicted velocity, then ask the model again from the new point: \(x_{t+h}=x_t+h\,v_\theta(x_t,t,c)\). The sampler shortens the last step so it lands exactly on \(t=1\). Before using the model, I checked the sampler against a constant velocity field, where the answer is known: 50 steps from 0, 25 from 0.5, and 4 from 0.93 (the last one shortened to 0.01). Each run took exactly that many steps and ended at \(1-t_{\text{start}}\) to within \(10^{-6}\). 

@torch.inference_mode()
def euler_sample(x_start, t_start, step_size, velocity):
    trajectory = []
    x_t = x_start
    t = t_start
    while t < 1.0:
        dt = min(step_size, 1.0 - t)
        x_t = x_t + dt * velocity(x_t, t)
        t = t + dt
        trajectory.append((t, x_t))
    return x_t, trajectory

25 updates from the \(t=0.50\) input

Three denoisers, one input

The sharpest result is not the closest one

Close up, Euler sampling gives the crispest result: the cables and the X-bracing are sharper than in the one-step estimate. Yet by PSNR it is slightly farther from the original. The one-step estimate is the posterior mean, which is exactly what minimizes squared error, and softness is the price. Repeated small steps let the model commit, step by step, to one particular sharp image. That image is consistent with the input, but its sharp edges and fine texture are the model's own guess and do not line up with the original pixel for pixel. A sharp edge in the wrong place costs more squared error than a soft edge in the right one. The Gaussian blur loses on both counts: it keeps low-frequency noise, and it removes detail the learned model restores.

Part A 2.1

Classifier-free guidance

Classifier-free guidance evaluates the model twice at the same \(x_t\) and \(t\): once with the prompt and once with the empty string. It then extrapolates past the conditional prediction, \(v_{\text{cfg}}=v_c+w(v_c-v_u)\). At \(w=0\) this is ordinary conditional sampling, and the code checks that exactly. 

def cfg_velocity(x, t, condition, w, predict_velocity, null_condition=''):
    v_c = predict_velocity(x, t, condition)
    v_u = predict_velocity(x, t, null_condition)
    return v_c + w * (v_c - v_u)

assert torch.equal(cfg_velocity(x,.5,condition,0,predict_velocity,null_condition),
                   predict_velocity(x,.5,condition))

Cleaner, more typical portraits

With the same five noises, guidance turns every sample into a tidy frontal studio headshot: centered, sharp eyes, smooth skin, a plain backdrop. RMS contrast nearly doubles (0.17 to 0.31 on the saved images), and the backgrounds go to deep black, white, or saturated blue. The term \(v_c-v_u\) points toward what makes an image match the prompt more than an arbitrary image would. Scaling it by 7 exaggerates those cues.

What is lost

The odd unguided samples are gone, and so is their variety of pose, age, and framing. Every guided sample has the same composition. Pixel colors still differ between seeds, but the range of images has narrowed to the most prompt-typical corner of the distribution. The seed fixes less than the prompt does. For seeds 114 and 117 the light background and the young man carry over from the unguided run, but for 115, 116 and 118 guidance changes the subject itself, from a man or an older woman to a younger woman. The subject bias stays. Guidance sharpens the model's idea of a high quality photo; it does not broaden it.

Part A 3.1

Image-to-image editing

SDEdit-style editing starts the sampler partway. The input is moved back to time \(t_{\text{start}}\) with fresh noise, then integrated to \(t=1\) with guidance and the generic prompt. The starting time sets how much of the input survives. At \(t_{\text{start}}=0.02\) the input is 98% noise and only its coarsest layout and color remain. At 0.5 only fine texture is replaced. All edits share one noise tensor (seed 114), so differences across a row come from \(t_{\text{start}}\) alone. 

def edit(image, noise, t_start, condition, predict_velocity, w=7, step_size=0.02):
    x_t = interpolate(image, noise, t_start)
    x, _ = euler_sample(x_t, t_start, step_size, lambda x, t: guided(x, t, condition, w))
    return x

Part A 3.3

Visual anagrams

A visual anagram looks like one thing upright and another rotated 180°. At each Euler step the model predicts a guided velocity for the current image with the first prompt. It also predicts one for the rotated image with the second prompt, and that prediction is rotated back. The step follows the average. Both readings are denoised together, so the final image satisfies both. Rotation by 180° flips both spatial axes, and the code asserts it is its own inverse.

The prompt pairs are not the ones on the assignment page. Waterfall / teddy bear and duck / rabbit come from the example prompts in the Visual Anagrams repository. There, the first is a three-view illusion and the second a square-hinge illusion; here both are 180° rotations. Each pair ran with three seeds, and each row shows the best one. 

def rotate180(x):
    return torch.flip(x, (-2, -1))

def visual_anagrams(noise, condition_a, condition_b, predict_velocity, w=7, step_size=0.02):
    def anagrams_velocity(x, t, c_a=condition_a, c_b=condition_b, w=w):
        v_cfg = guided(x, t, c_a, w)
        v_flip = rotate180(guided(rotate180(x), t, c_b, w))
        return (v_cfg + v_flip) / 2
    x, _ = euler_sample(noise, 0, step_size, anagrams_velocity)
    return x

The strongest pair. Two dark plums become the cat's eyes, the red apple its nose and mouth, and the yellow fruit between them the bridge of its nose. The bowl's rim becomes the brow. The only parts that do not belong to both readings are two pink triangles at the bowl's base: upright they look like stray shadows, rotated they are the cat's ears.

The white water streaked down the rock becomes the bear's bib, and the golden rocks become a paw. The teddy bear is the stronger reading. Upright, the waterfall works, but the bear's dark nose and eyes are still visible upside down in the foam.

A third pair, from the same source, with a different strategy. The duck's body becomes the rabbit's head, the dark mark on its wing the rabbit's eye, and its orange feet the ears. The duck's head has no role in the rabbit, so it hangs below the rabbit's chin as an upside-down leftover. Watercolor can leave most of the paper blank, so the model only has to share one compact shape.

Why averaging works

Each velocity is a direction of improvement for one reading. Their average improves both as long as they do not pull the same pixels in opposite directions, and early in sampling, when only coarse layout is being decided, there is a lot of room. The best results reuse regions: a plum is a plausible fruit and a plausible eye, so one dark disc satisfies both prompts. Pairs work when the two subjects share a silhouette (a round bowl and a round face) and when both prompts name the same medium, so neither reading has to fight over texture or palette. When no region can serve both, the average settles for a compromise. A piece of one subject survives as a small, out-of-place detail in the other reading: the bear's nose in the waterfall, the duck's head under the rabbit. My first attempt, ink-wash mountains / a young woman, failed in a different way. Ink wash leaves most of the paper blank, and on all three seeds neither subject got enough marks to read.

Part A 3.4

Hybrid images

Factorized diffusion builds a hybrid image inside the sampler. Two guided velocities are computed for the same \(x_t\). The low frequencies of one prompt's velocity are combined with the high frequencies of the other's: \(v=f_{\text{low}}(v_1)+v_2-f_{\text{low}}(v_2)\). The low-pass is a Gaussian with σ = 16 and a 265-pixel kernel at 512 × 512, the same idea as the hybrid images in Project 2. The difference is that the split now happens to the velocity at every step, not to two finished photographs.

I chose my own prompt pairs instead of the assignment's. Each ran with three seeds, and each case below shows the best one. 

def lowpass(x, sigma=16.0):   # provided: kernel 265 at 512 x 512
    kernel = 2 * round(16.5 * x.shape[-1] / 64) + 1
    return gaussian_blur(x, [kernel, kernel], [sigma, sigma])

def make_hybrids(noise, condition_a, condition_b, predict_velocity, w=7, step_size=0.02):
    def hybrid_velocity(x, t, c_a=condition_a, c_b=condition_b, w=w):
        v_dis = lowpass(guided(x, t, c_a, w))
        v_b = guided(x, t, c_b, w)
        v_near = v_b - lowpass(v_b)
        return v_dis + v_near
    x, _ = euler_sample(noise, 0, step_size, hybrid_velocity)
    return x

Part B 1.1

A UNet for MNIST

Part B trains everything from scratch on MNIST, with pixels in \([0,1]\). The network is a small UNet. Two stride-2 convolutions take the 28 × 28 input down to 7 × 7, average pooling squeezes it to a single vector, and the decoder mirrors the encoder. Skip connections concatenate each encoder level onto the decoder at the same resolution. Every convolutional block is Conv, BatchNorm, and GELU, and Flatten is average pooling plus GELU. Downsampling is a 3 × 3 convolution with stride 2, upsampling a 4 × 4 transposed convolution with stride 2. The bottleneck is a 7 × 7 average pool down and a 7 × 7 transposed convolution back up. 

def forward(self, x):
    e1 = self.enc1(x)                       # ConvBlock(1, D)        D x 28 x 28
    e2 = self.enc2(e1)                      # DownBlock(D, D)        D x 14 x 14
    e3 = self.enc3(e2)                      # DownBlock(D, 2D)      2D x  7 x  7
    f = self.flatten(e3)                    # AvgPool2d(7) + GELU   2D x  1 x  1
    d1 = self.unflatten(f)                  # ConvTranspose2d(k=7)  2D x  7 x  7
    d1 = torch.cat([d1, e3], dim=1)
    d2 = self.dec1(d1)                      # UpBlock(4D, D)         D x 14 x 14
    d2 = torch.cat([d2, e2], dim=1)
    d3 = self.dec2(d2)                      # UpBlock(2D, D)         D x 28 x 28
    d3 = torch.cat([d3, e1], dim=1)
    d4 = self.dec3(d3)                      # ConvBlock(2D, D)
    return self.out(d4)                     # Conv2d(D, 1, 3)

Part B 1.2

Training a single-step denoiser

The first task is plain denoising: given \(z=x+\sigma\epsilon\), predict \(x\), with an L2 loss. Each row below adds one noise level to the same ten training digits, with a single \(\epsilon\) shared across rows.

The denoiser is trained at σ = 0.5 with \(D=128\), batch size 256, and Adam at \(10^{-4}\) for 5 epochs. The noise is drawn fresh for every batch, after the dataloader, so the network never sees the same noisy digit twice. 

for epoch in range(num_epochs):

        optimizer.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); optimizer.step()

The same ten test digits and the same noise after each epoch. After epoch 1 every digit is legible, but faint speckles remain in the background, the thin 1 has a stray crossbar, and the loop of the 5 is a grey smudge. By epoch 5 the background is black and the 1 is a single stroke. The 5 is still smudged. Its noisy input really is ambiguous, so the MSE-trained network outputs a grey average of the plausible strokes.

Out-of-distribution noise levels

The same σ = 0.5 denoiser, applied to one test digit at every noise level, with one shared \(\epsilon\):

Generalizing below and above the training noise

Below σ = 0.5 the denoiser does fine: less noise means an easier version of the same task, and at σ = 0 it simply returns the digit. Above 0.5 the outputs hold up for a while. At 0.6 and 0.8 the 7 is still clean apart from a few faint specks. At 1.0 the noise is as strong as the digit itself. A grey haze surrounds the 7, and a clump of noise becomes a short extra bar across its stem. The network learned how much noise to expect, so it reads the extra noise as digit structure. The flow model in Part 2 avoids this by training on every noise level at once and being told which one it is facing.

Denoising pure noise

Now the input is pure noise, \(z=\epsilon\), independent of the target digit. A fresh \(D=128\) UNet is trained with the same settings for 5 epochs.

What patterns appear, and why

  • Every output is the same image. Ten different noise inputs give ten identical blurry blobs, an overlay of many digits with a bright core. The pattern is already final after epoch 1.
  • The blob is the training set's centroid. Since \(z\) is independent of \(x\), the input tells the network nothing about the target. For any fixed output \(y\), the expected loss \(\mathbb{E}_x\lVert y-x\rVert^2\) is minimized by \(y=\mathbb{E}[x]\), the pixel-wise mean of all training digits. The network learns to ignore its input and output that constant. The mean image I computed from the dataset (above) matches the outputs. Of the class means, 5, 8, and 9 lie closest to it.
  • The loss floor is the data's variance. Predicting the mean leaves the average per-pixel variance of MNIST, which I measured as 0.0673, exactly where the curve flattens. Predicting all-black would cost 0.112. No input-independent output can do better. This is the extreme case of the blurry one-step estimates in Part A: with no information, the conditional mean is the global mean.

Part B 2.1–2.3

Flow matching with a time-conditioned UNet

The OOD test broke down at σ = 1.0 because the denoiser knew only one noise level. The pure-noise test showed that a single step from noise can only reach the mean. Flow matching addresses both. It trains on the whole path \(x_t=(1-t)x_0+tx_1\), with \(t\sim U[0,1]\), tells the network the time, and samples with many small steps instead of one jump. The target is the path's velocity \(x_1-x_0\), not the clean image. A small fully connected block maps the scalar \(t\) to one channel vector that multiplies the bottleneck and another that multiplies the first decoder block. 

t1 = self.fc1_t(t)[:, :, None, None]        # FCBlock(1, 2D): Linear, GELU, Linear
t2 = self.fc2_t(t)[:, :, None, None]        # FCBlock(1, D)
d1 = self.unflatten(f) * t1
d2 = self.dec1(torch.cat([d1, e3], dim=1)) * t2

def time_fm_forward(unet, x_1, num_ts):
    t = torch.rand(N, device=x_1.device)
    noise = torch.randn_like(x_1)
    x_t = t_img * x_1 + (1 - t_img) * noise
    return F.mse_loss(unet(x_t, t), x_1 - noise)

def time_fm_sample(unet, img_wh, num_ts, seed=0):   # 50 Euler steps from noise
    x_t = torch.randn(N, C, H, W, device=device)
    dt = 1 / num_ts
    for i in range(num_ts):
        x_t = x_t + dt * unet(x_t, torch.full((N,), i * dt, device=device))
    return x_t

Training uses \(D=64\), batch size 64, and Adam starting at \(10^{-2}\). An exponential schedule multiplies the learning rate by \(0.1^{1/10}\) after every epoch, so it reaches about \(1.3\times10^{-3}\) in the last of 10 epochs.

Plausible strokes, uncertain identity

After one epoch the samples are squiggles with the stroke width of digits but no identity. By epoch 5 roughly half are recognizable. By epoch 10 the strokes are clean and thin, but about a quarter of the samples are still shapes between classes: a 4 crossed with a 9, a 6 that does not close, a 2 that becomes a smile. The exact unconditional flow would still split cleanly between classes (try None with 50 steps in the demo above). But without a label, the field has to change direction sharply where samples decide between digits, and a small network trained for 10 epochs smooths over those boundaries. Samples that sit near one stall between classes. A class label removes that hard decision.

Part B 2.4–2.6

Class conditioning and guidance

Adding the digit's label removes that ambiguity. A one-hot class vector goes through two more FC blocks and scales the same two feature maps, while time now shifts them: \(c_1\odot\text{unflatten}+t_1\) and \(c_2\odot\text{up}_1+t_2\). With probability 0.1 the class vector is zeroed during training, so the same network also learns the unconditional velocity. Sampling uses that for classifier-free guidance with \(\gamma=5\) and 300 Euler steps. Training otherwise matches 2.2: \(D=64\), batch size 64, Adam at \(10^{-2}\) with the same exponential schedule, 10 epochs. 

c = F.one_hot(c, num_classes=self.num_classes).float()
if mask is not None:
    c = c * mask.unsqueeze(1)                      # mask == 0: unconditional
d1 = self.unflatten(f) * c1 + t1
d2 = self.dec1(torch.cat([d1, e3], dim=1)) * c2 + t2

mask = torch.rand(N, device=device) > p_uncond      # keep the label 90% of the time

for i in range(num_ts):                             # class_fm_sample, 300 steps
    t = torch.full((N,), i * dt, device=device)
    v_c = unet(x_t, c, t)
    v_u = unet(x_t, c, t, uncond_mask)
    x_t = x_t + dt * (v_u + guidance_scale * (v_c - v_u))

Conditioning fixes identity

Even after one epoch every grid column is the requested digit. The strokes are blotchy, but the classes are never in doubt, which the time-only model never managed. The label settles the class from the first step, and guidance with \(\gamma=5\) amplifies it, pushing each sample away from the unconditional average. Without a run at \(\gamma=1\) I can't separate the two effects. By epoch 10 the digits are clean, with natural variation in slant and thickness within each column.

Removing the scheduler

How I compensated for the missing scheduler

  • What the scheduler was doing. It starts large, so the first epoch makes fast progress, and decays by 10× so the last epochs take small steps and settle. Removing it while keeping \(10^{-2}\) would leave every late update large, and the weights would never settle.
  • A lower constant rate. I used \(10^{-3}\), close to where the scheduled run ends. It is small enough to converge without decay. It also avoids the scheduled run's loss spike at the start, and its epoch-1 samples are already cleaner.
  • AdamW with weight decay \(10^{-4}\). A mild regularizer that keeps the weights from drifting while the step size stays constant. Everything else is unchanged: 10 epochs, batch size 64, \(p_{\text{uncond}}=0.1\), the same seed and \(\gamma=5\).
  • Result. The epoch-10 grids are equally clean, and every digit is legible in both. Two of the forty no-scheduler samples carry a stray dot (a 7 and an 8). Possibly a sign that the constant step size never let the weights fully settle.

What I learned

Almost everything in this project comes back to one fact: a network trained with squared error predicts an average.

The one-step estimate is blurry because it averages every clean image consistent with the input. The pure-noise denoiser outputs the mean digit because noise rules nothing out. The time-only model produces ambiguous digits where its smooth approximation of the average blurs the split between classes. Euler sampling works because each small step asks for a new average from a point where fewer answers remain, so the average sharpens into a single sample. Guidance, anagrams, hybrids, and SDEdit are all ways of changing which average the model is asked for: with a stronger prompt, with two prompts and a rotation, with two prompts split by frequency, or from a starting point that already rules most answers out. The most practical lesson was that PSNR and visual quality disagree. Euler's sharp result scored below the blurry estimate, so judging these models takes both the numbers and the pictures.
